Spatially Periodic Reaction-Diffusion Equations

A problem-oriented guide to research on propagation, pulsating traveling fronts, and spreading speeds in spatially periodic media.

This page collects results on spatially periodic reaction-diffusion equations and organizes the literature by mathematical questions. Before treating individual topics, we fix a common equation, notation, and baseline assumptions. Specific results may require stronger hypotheses, which will be stated in the corresponding sections.

General Framework and Basic Assumptions

Unless a topic states otherwise, we consider a scalar reaction-diffusion equation in the whole space \(\mathbb{R}^N\):

\[ \partial_t u(t,x) = \nabla\!\cdot\!\big(A(x)\nabla u(t,x)\big) + b(x)\cdot\nabla u(t,x) + f(x,u(t,x)), \qquad (t,x)\in I\times\mathbb{R}^N. \tag{1} \]

Here \(t\) is time, \(x\in\mathbb{R}^N\) is the space variable, and \(u(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\) is the unknown scalar state. The time interval \(I\) depends on the problem: it may be \((0,\infty)\) for an initial-value problem, a finite interval for a local-in-time problem, or all of \(\mathbb{R}\) for an entire solution such as a traveling or transition front.

Spatial periodicity

There is a lattice \(\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^N\) such that, for every lattice vector \(k\in\mathcal{L}\),

\[ A(x+k)=A(x),\qquad b(x+k)=b(x),\qquad f(x+k,s)=f(x,s). \]

The periodicity cell and lattice are fixed for a given equation. Time-periodic media, nonperiodic heterogeneous media, and bounded spatial domains are extensions that require separate notation or assumptions.

Diffusion matrix

For the baseline uniformly parabolic framework, assume that \(A(x)\) is a measurable, bounded, symmetric matrix and that there are constants \(0<\lambda\leq\Lambda<\infty\) such that, for almost every \(x\) and every \(\xi\in\mathbb{R}^N\),

\[ \lambda |\xi|^2\leq \xi\cdot A(x)\xi \leq \Lambda |\xi|^2. \]

This is uniform ellipticity. It is a useful weak baseline for divergence-form equations; classical front results often impose additional smoothness on \(A\), while degenerate or nonuniformly elliptic diffusion lies outside this baseline.

Advection field

Assume initially that \(b(x)\) is measurable and bounded. Periodicity is imposed as above. Depending on the theorem, one may need Hölder or smoother coefficients. The sign convention for the drift varies in the literature; here the term is written as \(+b(x)\cdot\nabla u\).

Reaction term

Assume that \(f(x,s)\) is measurable in \(x\), continuous in \(s\), and locally Lipschitz in \(s\), uniformly in \(x\) on bounded ranges; for classical results, continuity or Hölder regularity in \(x\) is commonly added. A standard baseline equilibrium condition is \(f(x,0)=0\). Additional equilibria, sign conditions, or monostable/KPP, ignition, and bistable assumptions are topic-specific and will be stated when used.

Solutions, existence, and regularity

  • Initial-value problem: prescribe \(u(0,x)=u_0(x)\) and study the solution for later times. Well-posedness assumptions depend on the chosen solution class and the growth of \(f\).
  • Entire solutions: solve the equation for every \(t\in\mathbb{R}\). Pulsating traveling fronts and transition fronts are examples; they are not, in general, specified by data at one initial time.
  • Weak versus classical solutions: measurable uniformly elliptic coefficients can support weak-solution theories under suitable hypotheses. To obtain classical derivatives and apply pointwise front arguments, stronger coefficient regularity is generally needed. Local parabolic regularity gives smoothness only to the extent allowed by the coefficients and nonlinearity.
  • Bounds: many front problems seek solutions between two equilibria, often normalized as \(0\) and \(1\). The bound \(0\leq u\leq1\) and the equilibrium condition \(f(x,1)=0\) are common in particular models, but are not universal assumptions for every problem on this page.

These are common baseline assumptions, not a claim that one set of hypotheses is minimal for every theorem. Each topic below must specify any additional assumptions needed for existence, uniqueness, speed formulas, stability, or asymptotic spreading.

1. Existence of Pulsating Traveling Fronts

1.1. Problem and assumptions

We study equation (1) in the whole space \(\mathbb{R}^N\), with the spatial periodicity and baseline ellipticity/regularity assumptions stated in the preceding section. To state a representative existence result, we make the following additional assumptions. They are sufficient hypotheses for the classical front theory described below; they are not claimed to be the weakest possible assumptions.

  • (F1) Periodic coefficients. The matrix \(A\), drift \(b\), and reaction \(f\) are periodic with respect to the same lattice. For the classical formulation, assume the coefficients are sufficiently regular (for example, \(A\in C^{1,\alpha}\), \(b\in C^{0,\alpha}\), and \(f\) is Hölder continuous in \(x\) and locally \(C^1\) in \(u\)), with the corresponding norms bounded on a periodicity cell.
  • (F2) Uniformly elliptic diffusion. The matrix \(A(x)\) is symmetric and there are constants \(0<\lambda\leq\Lambda\) such that \(\lambda|\xi|^2\leq \xi\cdot A(x)\xi\leq\Lambda|\xi|^2\) for all \(\xi\in\mathbb{R}^N\) and all \(x\).
  • (F3) Two equilibria. \(f(x,0)=f(x,1)=0\) for every \(x\).
  • (F4) Combustion (ignition) reaction. There is an ignition threshold \(\theta\in(0,1)\) such that \(f(x,s)=0\) for \(s\leq\theta\) and for \(s\geq1\), while \(f(x,s)>0\) for \(\theta

The hypotheses above describe one standard setting in which a clean existence-and-uniqueness theorem is available. The monostable/KPP and bistable cases require different reaction assumptions and are discussed separately in the literature; one should not infer their conclusions from the combustion case without checking those hypotheses.

1.2. Definition of a pulsating traveling front

Fix a unit direction \(e\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\) and a speed \(c>0\). A function \(u:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^N\to[0,1]\) is called a pulsating traveling front of equation (1), propagating in direction \(e\) with speed \(c\), if there exists a profile \(\Phi:\mathbb{R}^N\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]\) such that

\[ u(t,x)=\Phi\big(x,x\cdot e-ct\big), \qquad \Phi(x+k,s)=\Phi(x,s)\quad(k\in\mathcal{L}), \tag{2} \]

and the profile is nonincreasing in its second variable and connects the two equilibria uniformly in the periodic variable:

\[ \lim_{s\to-\infty}\Phi(x,s)=1,\qquad \lim_{s\to+\infty}\Phi(x,s)=0, \quad\text{uniformly in }x\in\mathbb{R}^N. \tag{3} \]

In addition, \(u\) must solve equation (1) in the classical (or theorem-appropriate weak) sense. The representation (2) describes a front whose level sets move in direction \(e\) with effective speed \(c\), while the profile oscillates according to the periodic medium. Reversing the orientation of the profile gives an equivalent convention with the limits \(0\) and \(1\) interchanged.

1.3. A representative existence theorem

Theorem 1 (Berestycki–Hamel, 2002; combustion case). Assume the periodic whole-space setting above and the standard smoothness, uniform ellipticity, and combustion-reaction hypotheses (F1)–(F4). Then for every prescribed direction \(e\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\), there exists a pulsating traveling front solving (1)–(3). In the combustion setting treated in the paper, the propagation speed is unique, the front is monotone in time, and the profile is unique up to translation in time (equivalently, a shift of the traveling coordinate), under the hypotheses of the theorem.

Reference: H. Berestycki and F. Hamel, “Front propagation in periodic excitable media,” Communications on Pure and Applied Mathematics, 55(8) (2002), 949–1032, doi:10.1002/cpa.3022 [3]. Their paper treats a broader class of periodic domains and coefficients. For biological invasion models in periodically fragmented habitats, see H. Berestycki, F. Hamel and L. Roques, “Analysis of the periodically fragmented environment model: II—Biological invasions and pulsating travelling fronts,” Journal de Mathématiques Pures et Appliquées, 84(8) (2005), 1101–1146 [5].

1.4. Scope and distinctions

  • KPP/monostable reactions: fronts can exist for a range of speeds, typically \(c\geq c^*(e)\); the minimal speed is characterized by a spectral variational formula under KPP hypotheses. See Berestycki–Hamel–Nadirashvili (2005), Section 2 below [4].
  • Bistable reactions: existence and speed selection depend on different sign and stability assumptions. The combustion theorem above does not automatically apply.
  • Initial-value versus entire solutions: a pulsating front is an entire solution, defined for all \(t\in\mathbb{R}\). It is not itself an initial-value problem, although its profile can describe the large-time behavior of solutions to suitable initial data.

2. Spreading Speeds and Minimal Speeds

Main question How fast does an invading solution spread, and can the minimal speed of a pulsating front be computed from a linear eigenvalue problem?

For KPP-type reactions, the leading edge of the invasion is governed by the equation linearized near the unstable state. In periodic media, the relevant linear operator has periodic coefficients. Its principal eigenvalue leads to a variational formula for the minimal front speed. This is a central distinction between KPP-type problems and general monostable or bistable equations, where linear determinacy need not hold in the same way.

Berestycki, Hamel & Nadirashvili (2005) — the periodic KPP speed formula

The speed of propagation for KPP type problems. I: Periodic framework. Journal of the European Mathematical Society, 7(2), 173–213.

This paper proves that the minimal speed of pulsating fronts for KPP-type equations in periodic media is given by a variational formula involving principal eigenvalues of suitable linear operators. It also studies how the speed depends on the geometry of the domain and on reaction, advection, and diffusion coefficients, and connects front speeds with asymptotic spreading speeds for the Cauchy problem.

Publisher / DOI: 10.4171/JEMS/26

Berestycki, Hamel & Roques (2005) — minimal speed in biological invasion

Analysis of the periodically fragmented environment model: II—Biological invasions and pulsating travelling fronts. Journal de Mathématiques Pures et Appliquées, 84(8), 1101–1146.

This paper also belongs here: besides proving existence of pulsating invasion fronts, it characterizes the minimal speed for the periodic biological model. It is a useful bridge between the abstract spectral formula and an applied invasion model.

Publisher / DOI: 10.1016/j.matpur.2004.10.006

Xin (1993) — effective speeds and propagation blocking

Existence and nonexistence of traveling waves and reaction-diffusion front propagation in periodic media. Journal of Statistical Physics, 73, 893–926.

Xin's paper is relevant to the broader propagation question: when a traveling wave exists, its effective speed describes the propagation of suitable initial data. For bistable reactions, sufficiently strong heterogeneity can instead lead to the failure of wave propagation. Thus, the minimal-speed picture for KPP fronts should not be assumed to apply unchanged to all reaction types.

Publisher / DOI: 10.1007/BF01052815

Key distinction

  • Minimal front speed: the smallest speed for which a pulsating traveling front exists in a given direction.
  • Asymptotic spreading speed: the speed describing the large-time expansion of solutions from suitable initial data.
  • In periodic KPP settings these quantities are closely linked and can be characterized spectrally; in other reaction regimes, the relationship requires separate hypotheses and results.
